芎林

an old Hakka township tucked into the Hsinchu hills

竹塹丘陵間的客家老庄

Overview總覽

Qionglin is a small Hakka township in the hills of Hsinchu County, Taiwan, named after the groves of crape-myrtle trees that once lined its riverbanks. This site is a slow walk through that history — its old temple street, its founding families, its orchards, and the everyday life that still fills it today. Start wherever you like, and explore at your own pace.

History & Origins歷史源流

Before it became today's Qionglin, this valley was first a forest, then a settlers' word for that forest. Four chapters trace how the same stretch of land grew from woodland, to hamlet, to township.

Origins of the name名字的由來

A forest of crape myrtle九芎成林

The banks of the Youluo River were once covered in dense groves of jiu-qiong trees — a hardy, smooth-barked species suited to dry, windy ground. The earliest settlers named their settlement after it: Jiuqionglin, "the crape-myrtle forest." Over the generations the name was worn down to today's simpler Qionglin. Two centuries-old crape myrtle trees still stand at the gate of Wenlin Pavilion, planted so later generations would remember where the name came from.

1775

The settling of the land

During the Qianlong era, Hakka pioneers from Chaozhou and Jiaying in Guangdong — among them the Jiang and Liu families — pushed inland along the Youluo River and began clearing farmland. Within a single generation, the settlement had grown large enough to need a guardian of its own.

Image圖片

The two crape myrtle trees still standing at the gate of Wenlin Pavilion — planted so the story behind Qionglin's name would never be forgotten.

1800

The Three Mountain Kings arrive廣福宮

Local gentry raised funds for a temple to the Three Mountain Kings, a guardian deity carried over from their home villages in Guangdong, and named it Guangfu Temple. By the early 1800s, most of the flatland along the river had been turned into fields, and Jiuqionglin had become a busy waypoint between the coast and the mountain frontier.

1876

A town that valued learning文林閣

In the 1870s, a local scholar founded a private study hall here, which by 1876 had grown into Wenlin Pavilion, a temple to the god of literature. At its height, the district is said to have supported twenty to thirty small schoolhouses — an unusually dense concentration of learning for a rural Hakka township. Wenlin Pavilion still quietly funds local schools today, more than a century after its first students arrived.

Today今日

From hamlet to township

Through Qing rule, Japanese colonial administration, and the postwar era, the settlement's boundaries shifted and its name was simplified, eventually settling into the twelve villages that make up Qionglin Township today. Home to roughly 20,000 people across 41 square kilometres, it remains overwhelmingly Hakka — more than 95 percent of the population — even as its southern edge grows into a quiet satellite community for workers commuting to the Hsinchu Science Park.

Secondary Forest老街古蹟

The old street beside Guangfu Temple is where Qionglin's history is easiest to picture. Its timber and brick shophouses are still standing, and a handful of the trades that once served the whole district haven't quite left.

Walk it slowly, and a blacksmith's forge, a tofu press, and a rice shop still open their doors along the lane. Follow it far enough, and the whole township's pace seems to slow down with you.

Guangfu Temple廣福宮

Built in 1800 and dedicated to the Three Mountain Kings, it remains the busiest temple in the township, drawing worshippers from seven villages.

Wenlin Pavilion文林閣

Founded in 1876 as a temple to the god of literature, it still funds local schools and welcomes students before exam season.

Chenggan Bridge呈甘橋

A wooden footbridge from 1818, rebuilt in stone in 1923. Its deck often doubles as a drying rack for pickled mustard greens.

Feifeng Mountain & the Youluo River飛鳳山．油羅溪

The ridgeline gives the township its best-known view; the riverbed below is scattered with dark basalt and smooth, rounded stones.
